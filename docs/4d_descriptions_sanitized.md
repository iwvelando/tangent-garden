# Four-dimensional space in *Death's End*

Descriptions of how three-dimensional beings experience four-dimensional space in part of the novel, followed by modeling notes. Paragraphs marked **Modeling note** are mathematical interpretations offered as starting points for visualization, not claims from the book. "The pursuing ship" and "the pursued ship" are the two human ships in these scenes.

## Part I: Four-dimensional space seen from three dimensions

### 1. The warped point: an invisible sphere that removes whatever it contains

This is the main phenomenon seen from three-dimensional space.

- A round opening about **one meter across** appears in a metal cabin wall where
  a picture had hung. Its edge is **perfectly smooth and reflective, like a
  mirror**.
- The removed region is not a hole in a flat wall but a **ball**:
  - The top of a cabinet behind the opening is also missing, cut off by the same
    mirror-like surface.
  - Together, the cut surfaces "looked like a portion of the inside of a
    sphere". The narration describes "some invisible knife" cutting through the
    cabinet and the folded clothes inside it.
- Through the opening, a sleeping person in the next cabin has **lower legs
  missing**. The cut surface is smooth and mirror-like, "as though covered by a
  layer of mercury", yet muscle and bone can be seen through it. The person is
  unharmed and keeps breathing.
- The cut surfaces of the bed and the legs **fit the same spherical surface** as
  the wall opening. The text calls it "a bubble-shaped space about a meter in
  diameter, which erased everything within its path".
- **Continuity through the missing region:** a violin bow pushed into the bubble
  loses its tip from view, but **the bow hair stays taut**. When pulled back, the
  bow is undamaged. Whatever lies inside the ball is still
  physically connected to what lies outside it.
- **Flow through the missing region:** pipes pass through a
  one-meter bubble with **sections missing in the middle**. Liquid flows along
  one pipe, disappears, and **reappears in the matching section on the other
  side**. The missing lengths differ from pipe to pipe but together "describe a
  spherical space".
- The bubble is **completely invisible** in three-dimensional space because it
  does not bend light there. It can only be detected when objects inside it
  disappear.
- **Detection by instruments:** warped points give off faint electromagnetic
  radiation with a distinct spectral signature that ordinary ship sensors can
  locate. In that region, a volume the size of a ship typically holds one or two;
  the most found at once was twelve.
- **Movement over time:** warped points stay stable only briefly, then drift away
  or disappear. One is seen to "move slowly and … shrink" until it
  vanishes about ten meters away. Most are about a meter
  across, but some are much larger.

**Modeling note.** Treat three-dimensional space as the hyperplane `w = 0` in
ℝ⁴. A warped point can then be modeled as a ball `B³(c, r)` inside that
hyperplane where the local geometry is bent, or where objects are pushed along
`w`, so the ball no longer overlaps ordinary 3D space.

A 3D observer sees the complement: everything outside the ball, with every
object that crosses the ball's boundary sphere `S²` cut cleanly along it.

- The bow hair staying taut and the liquid reappearing both say the same thing:
  the objects stay connected, and only their slice at `w = 0` is missing.
- A visualization could render ordinary 3D scenes (pipes, furniture, a limb) and
  subtract a sphere, drawing the cut faces as mirror-like spherical caps.

### 2. A body passing through a warped point

- Someone dives headfirst into the bubble "as though diving into a pool" and
  disappears from head to toe. As they do, **the cross-section of the body on the
  bubble's surface rapidly changes shape**. The mirror-like edge throws rippling
  reflections onto nearby walls.
- Afterward, **two hands and forearms emerge from the bubble and hang in
  midair**, attached to nothing visible, and pull the other two people in.
- Several people reach into the bubble and watch their hands and arms vanish,
  but **feel nothing**.

**Modeling note.** This is the Flatland scene of a sphere passing through a
plane, one dimension higher. As a body moves through the boundary sphere, the
visible part is the body minus the ball, and the cut is the body's intersection
with a moving spherical surface. An animation could sweep a mesh through a
sphere and render only the part outside it, with the changing intersection
contour highlighted.

### 3. A large-scale warp: part of a ship missing

- Seen from a small craft outside, the pursuing ship, normally "a perfect
  cylinder", has **a tail that ends in an inclined plane**. It is "about 20%
  shorter" than it should be, as if "a giant invisible knife had cut off" the
  tail.
- Nothing fails on board. Power keeps flowing and no alarms sound, although
  losing the tail should cause catastrophic failure.
- After the observer counts to thirty, the whole ship is visible again.
- Later, people on the other ship recall seeing **the rear third** of the
  pursuing ship warp into four-dimensional space for several minutes. The two accounts give different fractions (20% and a third),
  and neither is preferred here.

**Modeling note.** A cylinder cut by a tilted plane has an elliptical cut face.
If the warped region is very large compared with the ship, the part of its
boundary sphere that the ship crosses is nearly flat, which would match an
"inclined plane".

### 4. Micrometeoroids that pass through intact outer walls

- A **carbon-fiber nutrient tube** deep inside the ship, tens of meters from the
  nearest outer hull, is ruptured with signs of high-temperature ablation. Tiny
  black fragments a few microns across are embedded in the opposite wall of the
  tube.
- The ceiling and the dozens or hundreds of bulkheads between the tube and space
  are **unbroken**, and no breach alarm sounds. The damage implies an impact at
  about **30,000 meters per second**, which could not have been produced inside
  the ship. When it happens, the event is left unexplained.
- Later, once the ships are inside the four-dimensional region:
  - Micrometeoroid strikes increase. In one, a 140-nanometer particle destroys a
    reactor's magnetic-confinement controller.
  - The narration explains: "In three-dimensional space, the critical parts of
    the ships were wrapped in layers of protection, but here, they lay exposed
    to the fourth dimension, completely defenseless".

**Modeling note.** A shell of armor in 3D encloses its contents only within that
3D slice. Something approaching from the `w` direction does not need to cross
the shell, just as a point above a plane can reach the inside of a drawn circle
without crossing its outline.

### 5. You can't see into four dimensions from three, but you can be seen

- "It's not possible to look into the fourth dimension from three-dimensional
  space, but it is possible to be in four-dimensional space and see everything
  happening in three-dimensional space, and to affect things in it".
- A small party is described entering the other ship through a warped
  point to scout it. Separately, a crew member of the
  pursuing ship reports passing, in a corridor, a person from the pursued ship
  who has vanished by the time he turns around. Whether these
  describe the same event is an inference; the text does not state it.

### 6. The fragment as a whole: size, shape, and "projection"

- The region where space has four dimensions is called a **four-dimensional
  fragment**.
- A character warns that giving the size of four-dimensional space from three
  dimensions "is kind of meaningless". Only the **three-dimensional projection**
  of the fragment can be discussed. Early work suggests that projection is
  **spherical**, with a **radius of 40–50 AU** (astronomical units), about the
  size of the solar system.
- In three-dimensional space the region looks **empty and ordinary**. Its edge is invisible, "placid like the surface of a deep pool".

### 7. The fragment shrinks, and four-dimensional matter becomes lines

- Everyone in four-dimensional space suddenly finds themselves back in three
  dimensions without passing through a warped point. The ships have left the
  fragment about 20 hours earlier than calculated.
- The crews consider two explanations: the fragment is moving, or it is
  **shrinking**. They judge shrinking more likely.
- At the fragment's edge, **luminous lines** appear:
  - They are very thin and very straight, with **no visible thickness**, and
    **5,000–30,000 km long**.
  - Each one appears suddenly, glows blue, shifts to red, then curves, breaks
    into pieces, and vanishes.
  - They appear along the boundary "as though a giant pen constantly marked out
    the boundary".
- A probe finds hydrogen, helium, and some iron and silicon dust where a line
  had been.
- The scientists' conclusion: as the fragment shrinks, four-dimensional matter
  drops into three-dimensional space and **decays instantly**. The decay
  "flattened the fourth dimension, causing their volume to increase greatly and
  expand into the form of straight lines". A few tens of grams of
  four-dimensional matter could form a line nearly **10,000 km** long.
- When a large four-dimensional ring crosses the receding boundary:
  - "The universe seemed to be cut in half", like a star "pulled into a line in
    an instant", or a line drawn across the universe with a T-square.
  - The line is **close to one AU** long. The transcript gives the figure as
    "about 130 million kilometers"; I haven't checked that number by ear. Unlike
    the earlier lines, it has a visible thickness.
  - It cools from bluish-white to red, twists, and breaks into a **dust belt**
    lit silver by starlight, which looks like the Milky Way behind it.
  - Its internal four-dimensional structures are "annihilated instantaneously".

**Modeling note.** One way to picture "flattening the fourth dimension into a
straight line" is to take a small 4-volume, keep its measure roughly fixed, and
send its extent along `w` to zero. The lost extent then reappears as enormous
length in a single direction inside the 3D slice, like a very thin 4D slab
unrolled into a 1D filament. This mapping is my illustration, not the book's
mechanism.

A visualization could show a compact 4D object, such as a 4-ball or a
duocylinder, flattened frame by frame into a long, glowing, fraying line.

### 8. Why a higher dimension decays

- In this part of the novel, higher-dimensional space existing at large scale
  "decayed toward lower dimensions as inevitably as water fell over a cliff".
  That decay is "the root cause" of the fragment shrinking.
- The lost dimension is not destroyed. It "simply curled up from the macroscopic
  to the microscopic and became one of the seven dimensions folded up within the
  quantum realm".
- This links back to an earlier conversation, in which a character describes the string-theory
  picture: excluding time, ten dimensions, with only three accessible at
  macroscopic scale and the others "folded up in the quantum realm".
  - He calls the universe's speed-of-light limit (300,000 km/s) and its three
    macroscopic dimensions the **"three and three hundred thousand syndrome"**.
  - He argues that because light cannot cross the universe, the universe is like
    a paralyzed body whose brain cannot reach its limbs.
  - The same syndrome is named again when the fragment is discovered.

**Modeling note.** A standard picture is a cylinder `ℝ³ × S¹` whose circle
radius shrinks to zero. A large circular fourth dimension curling down to
subatomic size is the Kaluza–Klein way to show a dimension becoming "compact".

## Part II: Being inside four-dimensional space as a three-dimensional being

### 9. The Flatland analogy, and why "seeing through" is the wrong phrase

- The book's analogy: flat beings in a two-dimensional picture see only the
  **edges** of the world, as line segments. Only when lifted into 3D and looking
  down do they see the whole picture.
- In the same way, a 3D person looking back from 4D realizes "he had never seen
  the world while he was in it". What they had seen before was "just a narrow
  view from the side… A line". Only from four dimensions is the picture seen whole.
- **Nothing hides anything behind it:** "Even the interiors of sealed spaces
  were laid open".
- The book stresses that **nothing is being seen through**. It uses the image of
  looking at a circle drawn on paper, where you see its inside "without looking
  through anything".
- **Perspective still applies:** distant objects look indistinct, "but
  everything was visible".
- The ship looked like "a magnificent, immense painting that had just been
  unrolled", visible from bow to stern: every cabin, every sealed container, the
  liquid in the pipes, the fusion fireball in the reactor.

**Modeling note.** In 3D we see a 2D image of a 3D world, and surfaces block what
is behind them. A 4D observer would have a 3D "retina", and a 3D object would
map onto it with every interior point unblocked, just as a 2D drawing lies fully
exposed to a 3D viewer.

The book doesn't explain how 3D-bodied humans with ordinary eyes perceive this.
It says only that they do, and that it is "indescribable".

A visualization could draw the same 3D object as an opaque surface render and
then as a fully transparent volume render showing every internal layer, labeled
"3D view" and "view from 4D".

### 10. Infinite detail: every cross-section at once

- One can see "the interior of solids, such as the bulkheads or a piece of metal
  or a rock… all the cross-sections at once".
- **"Unlimited details":** in 3D the visible elements are limited and can be
  taken in one by one. From 4D, all concealed details appear together because
  objects are "laid open at every level". In a sealed container you see not only
  the contents but also the inside of each thing in it.
- Even a cup or a pen shows "infinite details". A lifetime would not be enough
  "to take in the shape of any one of these objects in four-dimensional space".
- The result is "a vertigo-inducing sensation of depth, like a set of Russian
  nesting dolls that went on without end".
- **Bodies:** people see each other's bones, organs, marrow, blood moving
  through the chambers of the heart, the heart valves opening and closing, and
  the internal structure of each other's eye lenses.
- The book specifies that "**in parallel**" does not mean rearranged. Skin
  still encloses the organs, and the familiar 3D outline remains, but it is now
  "merely one detail among an infinity of details".

**Modeling note.**
- **Nested dolls:** repeatedly slicing an object and showing every slice at once
  gives a self-similar, recursive render, for example a volume shown as an
  infinite stack of cutaways.
- **"All cross-sections at once":** show an object's full family of plane
  sections side by side or as a scrollable sheaf, with the original shape still
  outlined.

### 11. The brain's adaptation: frames only

- At first, the flood of information threatens to shut down processing.
- The brain soon adapts and, without conscious effort, "learned to ignore most
  details, leaving only the frames around objects".

**Modeling note.** This suggests a practical way to draw 4D experience: begin
with a dense volume render that shows every layer, then fade it to a wireframe
or edge outline, the "frames".

### 12. An extra direction for touch

- In 4D, a hand can touch organs and internal parts directly. Doing so causes
  some pain or nausea and a risk of infection. Machines are just as exposed: a
  touch could hit live wires, steam, or circuits.
- People learn that **moving in one direction** grasps someone's hand, while
  touching the bones inside the hand takes "exertion in another direction. A
  direction that didn't exist in three-dimensional space".
- Handing over a small glass ecosphere, a person holds it carefully "by its
  three-dimensional edge", while every detail of the tiny ecosystem inside is
  visible.
- A pilot steers with voice and gaze and keeps hands off controls that now "lay
  exposed in four dimensions".

**Modeling note.** A 3D object is a thin slab in ℝ⁴. Every interior point lies on
the slab's boundary as seen from the `w` direction, so pushing in `±w` reaches
the inside. Pushing within the slab only contacts the outer 2D surface, as it
would in ordinary 3D.

### 13. Surrounded by space while still inside the ship

- While still inside the ship, breathing its air and wearing no spacesuits,
  people **see the stars in every direction**. "In the fourth dimension, they
  were exposed to space".
- Even during spacewalks they had a suit around them. Now "nothing stood between
  space and them". "In the fourth dimension, the ship lay in parallel with all of
  space".

### 14. High-dimensional spatial sense: depth at every point

- Once no longer overwhelmed by detail, people "sensed space itself, or sensed
  the fourth dimension". This comes to be called **high-dimensional spatial
  sense**.
- The book's attempt to describe it: vastness or boundlessness in 3D is
  "replicated an infinite number of times in four-dimensional space, in a
  direction that did not exist in three-dimensional space".
- **Hall of mirrors:** two facing mirrors produce an endless sequence of
  reflections; in the analogy, each reflected mirror is a whole 3D space. "The
  vastness one experienced in 3D space was but a cross-section of the vastness
  of four-dimensional space".
- The space looks "empty and uniform", yet has a depth that "was not a matter of
  distance. It was bound up in every point in space".
- The line later treated as a classic quote: "**A bottomless abyss exists in
  every inch**".
- The sensation grows stronger farther from the ships, deeper in four-dimensional
  space. The small craft's fusion flame, together with the reactor fires of the two
  larger ships, lights "this world of infinity times infinity".

**Modeling note.**
- **Stack of 3D copies:** ℝ⁴ = ℝ³ × ℝ is literally a stack of 3D spaces indexed
  by `w`.
- **"Every point" having depth:** each point `(x, y, z)` in the 3D slice has a
  whole line `{(x, y, z, w)}` running out of the slice.
- **Visual:** an infinite regress of nested translucent cubes, as in facing
  mirrors, or a tesseract-style "cube within a cube" repeated forever.

### 15. Three-dimensional space as a thin sheet of paper, and warped points as bends in it

- Returning from 4D, people find themselves exactly where they entered. "In fact,
  they had never left. The space they were in had just gained an extra
  dimension".
- "Four-dimensional space contains three-dimensional space, just as
  three-dimensional space contains two-dimensional space… we're located inside a
  three-dimensional sheet of paper in four-dimensional space".
- **The soap-bubble model**, proposed by a character: all of 3D space is "a
  large, thin sheet of paper, sixteen billion light-years across", and
  "somewhere on this sheet of paper is a tiny, four-dimensional soap bubble".
  Travelers crawl across the paper, reach the bubble, and at a warped point
  "leave the surface of the paper and enter the space inside the bubble".
- **What a warped point is:** "The three-dimensional sheet of paper is not
  completely flat everywhere. Some places are warped, reaching into the fourth
  dimension… a tunnel from lower dimensions into higher dimensions". Warped points are "everywhere" in the fragment.
- **Bodies stay three-dimensional:** "We were flat, three-dimensional people,
  drifting in four-dimensional space." How their bodies survive there, under
  probably different physical laws, remains unknown. A
  four-dimensional speaker later calls them "**thin pictures**… fragile".
- **Fragility of 3D things:** "In a sense, three-dimensionality is synonymous
  with fragility." Seen from 4D, even a very strong probe was "an unrolled
  painting, defenseless". Attackers approached from the fourth dimension and
  wrecked its internal mechanisms without understanding them.
- Asked whether this is the only such bubble, the same character argues that
  landing in the only 40–50 AU bubble on a sheet 16 billion light-years across is
  so improbable that there must be others.

**Modeling note.** This is the book's own picture and is easy to draw:
1. Show a thin 3D slab embedded in ℝ⁴, drawn one dimension lower as a 2D sheet
   in 3D.
2. Add a localized bubble, a small bulge of genuine thickness.
3. Add small dimples where the sheet bends out of its own plane: the warped
   points.

### 16. Seeing a warped point from inside 4D

- From within four-dimensional space, a warped point is visible because it
  **refracts light**:
  - It is usually spherical, and objects inside it look **distorted**, "causing
    a visual break" in their images.
  - The narrator says this is only an optical effect in four-dimensional space,
    not a real change in the objects' shapes.
- Pipes inside the sphere look curved and distorted, and the sphere looks "like a
  drop of dew hanging on a spider web".
- In 3D the same region is invisible and refracts nothing.

**Modeling note.** Render a spherical lens: a ball with a refractive index
different from its surroundings, set over a scene of straight lines such as
pipes or a grid. Straight features will bend and break at the sphere's edge,
like a dewdrop on a web.

### 17. Navigation blind in one coordinate

- Fixing a position needs **four coordinates**, but instruments brought from 3D
  can determine only **three**. So explorers "could not determine the location of
  any object in four-dimensional space with precision" and could collide with a
  target without warning.
- For a warped point, "all that was known was its **direction**, not its
  **distance**". Using one to return to 3D could leave a craft far from its ship. Novices are warned that a return trip could land them
  outside the ship in 3D space.
- Because of the extra dimension that can't be measured, "visual judgments of
  distance were completely unreliable". A target "might be as far away as an
  astronomical unit, or as close as the pinnace's bow".
- **Size changes in jumps, with no gradual approach:**
  - After hours of flight, the target ring stays "but a dot". Then suddenly it
    is "a ring the size of a coin… no gradual process of change at all".
  - Two hours later, about 200,000 km of equivalent 3D travel, it suddenly
    becomes a gigantic structure, and the craft barely avoids a collision,
    passing through the ring "like an arch in space".
  - "We're basically blind in the fourth dimension".
- Once the distance is known, the return trip takes half the time.
- **Signals weaken faster:** most radio waves between the ship and the small
  craft "would spill into the fourth dimension", making the signal fade much
  faster.

**Modeling note.**
- **Stepwise size change:** this can be read as projection with one depth
  coordinate hidden. The 3D image of a 4D object depends on its `w` offset,
  which the observer cannot sense, so apparent size need not change smoothly
  with any quantity the observer can measure. The book does not give a
  mechanism; it calls the observers "blind".
- **Signal falloff:** in n spatial dimensions, a point source's flux falls off
  as `1/r^(n−1)`. That is `1/r²` in 3D and `1/r³` in 4D, which fits the
  "spill" description.
- **Visual:** a slider hides the `w` coordinate while a 4D object's 3D shadow
  jumps between sizes.

### 18. Truly four-dimensional objects: sealed and infinitely layered

- The first 4D artifact seen through a telescope is a **ring**:
  - Its 3D diameter is estimated at 80–100 km and its band at about 20 km thick.
    Both are only guesses, because its distance cannot be measured.
  - It looks like "a giant wedding band spinning in space", with circuit-like
    markings.
- **Sealed:** unlike 3D objects, "it didn't reveal its interior". That is how it
  is identified as "a true four-dimensional object". A 4D
  object keeps its interior hidden from 4D viewers, the way a 3D box does from
  us.
- The ring emits no electromagnetic, neutrino, or gravitational-wave signals. It
  shows no acceleration beyond a slow, steady rotation.
- About **a hundred** other sealed 4D objects are found. More than a dozen can be
  resolved: **pyramids, crosses, polyhedral frames**, and irregular shapes built
  from simpler ones. The rest appear only as points.
- **Up close:**
  - The sealed ring gives "an immense sense of depth and of containment": "not
    just a ring, but an infinity of rings all stacked together in concealment".
  - This is called **high-dimensional materiality**, compared to seeing "the
    mountain contained in a mustard seed".
- Its surface is dark copper, and the lines that looked like circuitry are
  actually **scratches from micrometeoroids**.
- A speaker from the four-dimensional object states a principle across dimensions:
  - Lower-dimensional space cannot threaten higher-dimensional space.
  - Lower-dimensional resources are useless to higher-dimensional beings.
  - So hostility of the kind the book elsewhere calls "dark forest" happens only
    between those sharing the same number of dimensions.

**Modeling note.** Natural 4D analogues of the book's shapes:

| Shape in the book | 4D analogue |
|---|---|
| Ring | **Spherinder torus** or **ditorus** (a torus swept around a circle), or a **duocylinder** |
| Pyramid | **5-cell** (4-simplex), or a pyramid with a cube base |
| Cross | Tesseract-based cross |
| Polyhedral frame | Wireframes of the regular **4-polytopes**, such as the 8-cell (tesseract), 16-cell, 24-cell, or 120-cell |

"An infinity of rings stacked together" matches the family of 3D cross-sections
you get by sweeping a slicing hyperplane through a 4D torus: an animated sequence
of tori and tori-of-tori. It also matches the Clifford-torus decomposition of
the 3-sphere into nested tori.

## Part III: Emotional aftereffects with a geometric basis

- After returning, 3D space feels "narrow and smothering". One explorer feels
  claustrophobic, "as though he was being suffocated". Even a later spacewalk
  would feel confined.
- Researchers later spend almost all their time in 4D partly because they find
  the "confinement and claustrophobia" of 3D unbearable.
- Earlier, before anyone aboard the pursuing ship openly discusses warped
  points, one person already describes claustrophobia after a half-remembered
  "dream" of a place "open in a way you can't even imagine". The comparison he
  gives is someone raised in a small box who is let out and then put back. He also feels "squeezed between two iron plates until I'm flat as
  a sheet".
- Later, that same person is said to have experienced four-dimensional
  space once before, "in a semi-conscious state". He recalls
  being "only half awake" when the ship's rear section warped, and being
  persuaded it was a hallucination.

**Modeling note.** "Flat as a sheet" and "a small box" are the same image the
book uses for 3D space: a thin slab in ℝ⁴. A visualization could keep a 3D
scene constant while the space around it widens, or show a person first inside a
box, then in open 4D, then back in the box.

## Implementation notes: mapping these scenes onto a 4D scene model

This whole section is modeling interpretation, not book content.

A good general abstraction is an implicit field `F(x, y, z, w)`. You render the
3D slice `S_w = {(x, y, z) | F(x, y, z, w) ≤ 0}` and treat `w` as an animatable
parameter. That fits the book, but the book's scenes use **two different
viewpoints**, and each needs a different rendering strategy.

### A. The 3D observer: render the slice at `w = 0`

Represent ordinary objects as **thin slabs** in ℝ⁴, displaced along `w` by a
field `h`:

```
F_obj(x, y, z, w) = max( G(x, y, z),  |w − h(x, y, z)| − ε )
```

- `G` is the object's ordinary 3D signed distance function (SDF).
- `ε` is a small slab half-thickness.
- `h` is zero everywhere except inside warped points. For example,
  `h(p) = A · bump(|p − c| / r)`, with a smooth radial bump centered at `c` and
  a peak `A ≫ ε`.

What this gives you:
- **The warped point.** At `w = 0`, every object disappears wherever
  `|h| > ε`. That region is a ball when `h` is radial, so the cut faces all lie
  on one spherical surface: the mirror-smooth sphere the book describes.
- **Continuity through the gap.** The slab stays connected in ℝ⁴, so the taut
  bow hair and the pipe liquid that disappears and reappears need no special
  handling.
- **The missing ship's tail.** Use a large `h` region that overlaps the end of a
  cylinder. When the boundary sphere is large, the cut face is nearly a tilted
  plane.
- **Drift and shrink.** Animate `c` and `r` over time.
- **Topology mode.** The book supplies real examples:
  - A body diving through the sphere has a cut face that changes shape.
  - Forearms reaching back out of the bubble show up in the slice as pieces
    disconnected from any visible body.

  In both cases a single connected object in ℝ⁴ appears as separate or changing
  components at `w = 0`.
- **Sweeping `w` (fourth-axis navigation).** Moving the slice to `w ≈ A` shows
  the displaced material hidden inside the bubble. In the book, people in 3D
  cannot see this. The software can offer it as a view the characters don't
  have, and it should be labeled that way.

### B. The observer inside 4D: not a slice

In the book, someone inside 4D does not see a changing slice. They see the
**entire 3D world at once**, with every interior laid open.
- Render the 3D content with depth occlusion turned off, showing every internal
  layer and every cross-section at once. This is an "inside-out" or unoccluded
  mode.
- Over time, fade that into edges only, to match the book's "frames".
- Avoid presenting it as transparency or X-ray vision. The book insists that
  nothing is seen *through*; everything is simply exposed, like the inside of a
  circle drawn on paper.
- Keep the familiar outer shape visible as one detail among many, because the
  book says spatial arrangement is preserved.
- Distance along `w` can be the hidden parameter behind the stepwise size jumps
  (dot, then coin, then giant ring). Change it without giving the viewer any
  smooth visual cue.

### C. Genuinely 4D objects: sweep `w` through them

The sealed 4D artifacts are where classic slicing of 4D shapes applies. "An
infinity of rings stacked together" can be shown by sweeping a hyperplane
through a ditorus or duocylinder. Sweeping through a 5-cell, a tesseract, or
another 4-polytope covers the pyramids and polyhedral frames. A tesseract is a
reasonable stand-in for a "polyhedral frame", though the book never singles one
out.

### D. Decay into lines

Animate a small 4D object's extent along `w` down to zero while stretching it
along one 3D axis with its measure held roughly constant. Then fray and redden
the resulting filament.

### Framings to avoid for these scenes

- **"A window into 4D" or "looking through" to 4D.** In the book, a 3D observer
  cannot look into 4D at all. A warped point seen from 3D shows only absence:
  objects missing and smooth cut faces.
- **A bubble that exposes hidden surfaces inside itself.** In the book, exposure
  happens only once the observer has entered 4D. It then applies to the whole 3D
  world and to all of space, not just to the bubble's interior. From inside 4D,
  the bubble itself appears as a refracting "dewdrop" that distorts what is
  inside it.
