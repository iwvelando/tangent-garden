# A first spatial study

The artistic thread worth carrying into 3D is a simple curve giving rise to something unexpectedly intricate through a visible family of lines. A shaded solid alone would lose that connection. The construction should remain legible with its surface hidden.

## Candidates

| Construction                           | Visual opportunity                                                    | Mathematical and product tradeoff                                                                                |
| -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Tangent developable of a space curve   | Folded ribbons, sharp seams, intertwined sheets, line-only string art | Direct definition from a curve and its unit tangents; manageable first prototype                                 |
| Families of spatial involutes          | Unwound filaments surrounding a knot, with taut generating segments   | A natural continuation of existing involutes; needs careful arc-length integration and family framing            |
| Focal surfaces of normal congruences   | Layered cusps, ridges, and sheets around a surface                    | No single spatial analogue of a planar evolute; requires an explicit surface and principal-curvature conventions |
| Reflective/refractive caustic surfaces | Luminous folds, cusp edges, and concentrated ray families             | Needs surface normals and a two-parameter ray family; intensity would be a separate mathematical model           |

The first implementation chooses a **tangent developable of a space curve**, starting with torus knots. This offers actual spatial folds while retaining a base curve, straight construction lines, and a derived object. The gold/teal palette and notebook presentation continue the planar studies. Color varies periodically with the base parameter and shading communicates orientation; neither encodes physical light intensity or curvature magnitude.

## What to try

Choose **3D studies** in the notebook header, or open `?study=3d`. Switching to **2D studies** preserves both studies and their manual views. Start with Trefoil, orbit slowly, then hide **Ribbon surface** to see how straight tangents build the folds. Change **Tangent reach L** from a small positive value to 3 to watch those lines form broader intersecting sheets. Try Cinquefoil and Woven orbit for more intricate arrangements.

The torus presets use coprime windings (2,3), (2,5), and (3,4). R is the major torus radius, r its minor radius, and L the half-length of each unit-tangent segment. R lies in [0.1,20], r in [0.01,R), and L in (0,20]. Samples range from 240 to 2400 and representative tangent lines from 12 to 240.

Choose **Custom parametric** under Spatial definition to open a torus knot as editable expressions. Try the helix or spatial Lissajous presets for other shapes. A useful helix is `x=2*cos(t)`, `y=2*sin(t)`, `z=a*t/3`, from `-3*pi` to `3*pi`. Changing `a` changes its rise. Every geometric scalar and domain field accepts constant expressions through Go. Coordinate expressions bind `t` and `a`; scalar fields do not. Switching definitions preserves edited custom coordinates; choosing a preset replaces the study.

Drag to orbit, shift-drag to pan, and scroll to zoom; on a touch screen, pinch to zoom and move two fingers together to pan. Keyboard equivalents are arrows, shift-arrows, +/−, and Home. **Front**, **Side**, **Top** and **Isometric**, under the drawing, turn the camera to look along −z, along +x, down from just short of overhead, or from equal angles to all three axes, keeping zoom and pan; the axis indicator beside them shows which way x, y and z point as you orbit (see [named views](mathematics.md#named-views-and-the-orientation-indicator)). Try **A Lissajous knot, seen from three sides**: from the front, the side and the top it is three different Lissajous figures. **Rotate view** provides a quick camera-only preview. **Animation** offers a progressive reveal, simultaneous parameter tracks, a full camera orbit, a flight through key views (see [Flying the camera](#flying-the-camera)), or, for mirrors and interfaces, **Trace rays**. Pause to scrub; Stop restores the base study and manual view. Once an animation completes, orbit, pan, and zoom work on its final frame, starting from the animation camera; **Back to study** restores the base study and manual view. Four camera modes match the planar notebook; a fifth flies key views while the geometry moves (see [While the geometry moves](#while-the-geometry-moves)). Hold current view includes orbit, pan, and zoom in playback and exports.

## Involute filaments

Choose **Construction · Involute** to unwind taut strings from the same curves. Arc length starts at **Anchor t₀**, where the string has length **c**; the free end traces a filament that touches the curve in a cusp where the string runs out. **Family of involutes** replaces c with 2–24 evenly spaced lengths. Open **Unwinding a staircase** to see a helix shed nine stacked planar spirals, each a lifted circle involute, joined by the tangent strings. **A knot shedding filaments** unwinds seven close lengths from a (3, 4) knot into a braided cage. Hide **Unwinding strings** to see the filaments alone. Animate c, the anchor, or the family range and count; reveal grows the filaments from the domain start without re-measuring arc length. Arc length never crosses a pole or stationary point: the far side is left without a filament, with a note. See [the mathematical conventions](mathematics.md#spatial-involute).

## Tangent feet and half-turns

Choose **Tangent-foot projection** to drop perpendiculars from a fixed pole onto the curve's moving tangent lines. Choose **Tangent-line orthotomic** to extend each perpendicular the same distance beyond its foot: a half-turn of the pole around that tangent line. The pole has independent x, y, and z coordinates, all accepting constant expressions and parameter animation. Small crosses mark the tangent feet; a larger cross marks the pole. Toggle the derived curve, **Perpendiculars & tangent feet**, and **Pole marker** separately.

Open **A knot through perpendiculars** for the trefoil's tangent-foot curve, or **Half-turns around a helix** for the orthotomic. Move the pole and watch its image wrap around the original thread. A straight curve gives a single point image, shown as a cross. Invalid tangents and unresolved intervals leave gaps; regular components after a gap still have images. These are constructions on tangent lines, with no selected Frenet normal and no optical interpretation. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-tangent-foot-projection-and-orthotomic).

## Harmonic generators

Choose **Spatial definition · Harmonic sum** to add turning vectors: r(t) = c₀ + Σ[Aₖ cos(ωₖt) + Bₖ sin(ωₖt)]. Each term turns around the ellipse spanned by Aₖ and Bₖ at frequency ωₖ, and the vectors are chained from c₀ in term order. Grey chains show the vector sums at the representative samples; at the last one shown, darker lines repeat that chain and trace each term's generating ellipse around its joint, with a cross at c₀. Toggle **Vector sums** and **Generating ellipses** separately; they stay available under every construction. Up to eight terms; every field accepts constant expressions and parameter animation.

The domain is yours: the curve closes only when it spans a whole number of periods, and the note under the domain says so. **A harmonic trefoil** chains two circles turning opposite ways with a vertical wave into a trefoil knot. **Tilted ellipses · 1 : 3 : −5** tilts its generating ellipses into three different planes. **An orbit that never closes** uses frequencies 1 and φ, whose ratio is irrational: the ribbon stays an open arc however long the domain. Draw along the curve to watch the chain turn as the curve grows. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-harmonic-curves).

## Inversion in a sphere

Choose **Sphere inversion** to send every point along the ray from a center O to the point whose distance from O is R² divided by its own. The sphere of radius R stays fixed while its inside and outside trade places. **Curve to invert** chooses the base curve or its tangent-foot or orthotomic projection from the pole. **Center x**, **Center y**, **Center z**, and **Sphere radius R** accept constant expressions and parameter animation. Grey segments join each point to its image along their shared ray; three great circles and a small cross mark the sphere and its center. Toggle the **Inverted curve**, **Correspondence segments**, **Inversion sphere & center**, and, for a projection, **Projection & pole** separately.

Open **A staircase drawn into a sphere** to see every turn of a long helix pulled inside the sphere and coiled toward its center. **A trefoil turned inside out** inverts the knot in a sphere through its middle, and **Inverted perpendiculars** inverts the trefoil's tangent-foot curve. Move the center onto the curve and the image opens into branches that run off to infinity: they are never joined across it, and a note counts each passage through the center. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-sphere-inversion).

## Refining between samples

Every curve is drawn as chords between evenly spaced samples, and a chord can miss what the curve does between them. Check **Refine between samples** in **Sampling & definition** to add drawn points between samples wherever a chord strays from the curve by more than 1/5000 of its size. The surfaces, construction lines, framing, reveal and probe keep the evenly spaced samples; only the curve, a projection, an inverted curve, each involute, each offset strand, a ruled surface's partner thread, a canal's meridians and the base under a derived input are drawn more finely. A readout under the checkbox counts the points added, any gaps or jumps found between samples (which also break the surfaces there), and any piece left coarser than the tolerance. Refinement is not offered for vector fields, chases, surfaces, mirrors and level sets, and it stops at the finest step (1/1024 of a sample) and at 16,384 points per curve.

Open **A coil hidden between samples**: a harmonic trefoil carrying a coil of frequency 233, sampled 240 times. Uncheck the box and the coil vanishes. Each sample lands a little behind the last on the coil, so the samples trace a slow, smooth wave that is not there. **A rose that misses the center** inverts a five-petal rose whose petals pass just above the sphere's center; each near miss throws out a loop, which the samples cut into a polygon and refining draws round. **Strings swung round a three-cornered hat** unwinds four strings from a deltoid lifted at its corners, which stay regular but turn the tangent half a turn within a few samples: there every string's free end swings round a half circle, a few chords on the samples alone. **A string swept across a four-cornered crown** does the same for one string on a lifted astroid and lengthens it as it plays, refined in every frame. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-refinement-between-samples).

## Building on a derived curve

The developable, involute, framed ribbon, ruled surface and tube can be built on a derived curve rather than on the base. **Built on** chooses **The base curve**, **Its tangent-foot curve** (the feet H of perpendiculars from the pole P onto the tangents) or **Its tangent-line orthotomic** (P turned half a turn about each tangent, `2H − P`), and **Pole x**, **Pole y** and **Pole z** appear for a derived curve. The construction then acts on that curve: the legend names it, the probe describes it, and the base curve is drawn in grey beneath it with representative perpendiculars and the pole. **Base curve**, **Perpendiculars & tangent feet** and **Pole marker** are layers of their own, and the pole's coordinates are animation tracks. The tangent-foot and orthotomic constructions, sphere inversion and the curve alone have no such choice.

A pole on the curve stops its tangent-foot curve in a cusp at the pole: a construction on it is broken there, never joined across, and the note under the drawing counts each cusp. Open **Filaments off a cusp** to unwind a family of strings from a cardioid lifted into space, the tangent-foot curve of a saddle-shaped loop through its own pole: each filament runs out at the cusp from one side or the other. **A horn of perpendicular feet** threads a tube along the helix's tangent-foot curve, which flares as the tangent climbs while the helix stays inside it, and **Chords across a knot's reflection** strings chords along the cinquefoil's orthotomic from its center. Move the pole, or animate it, to watch the derived curve and everything built on it change together. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-constructions-on-a-derived-curve).

**Its involute** builds on the free end of a string unwound from the base instead: **Input anchor t₀** is where its arc length starts and **Input string c** its length there, independent of an involute construction's own, so you can unwind an involute from an involute. There is no pole; grey **Strings from the base** run along the base's tangents to the involute. The involute moves along the base's principal normal, so it has a cusp where the string runs out and stops wherever the base is straight. Open **Where a helix's tangents land**: every string unwound from the helix ends on one flat spiral, an involute of a circle, threaded with a thin tube. **A trefoil's string, unwound** follows the whole knot's involute spiralling outward, a full knot's length of string by the end. **A sheet of Viviani's normals** builds the tangent developable on an involute of Viviani's curve: its rulings run along that curve's principal normals, folding at the cusp where the string vanishes. Animate **Input string c** to watch the whole construction unwind. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#built-on-an-involute).

## Carried frames and twisted ribbons

A space curve's tangent leaves a whole plane of normal directions, so a ribbon along it needs a _frame_ to say which way it leans. Choose **Framed ribbon · offset strands**. The default **Rotation-minimizing** frame is carried along like a bead's orientation on a wire, never turning about the tangent unless the curve forces it. **Frenet** follows the curvature instead; keep it as a diagnostic, since it is undefined on a straight run and reverses at an inflection, where its ribbon breaks. At representative samples, short arms mark U (the longer) and V, with the tangent in grey. The ribbon leans along D, turned θ₀ from U and twisted a chosen number of turns over the curve; offset strands wind around the curve at distance d.

**A band around the trefoil** carries a twisted band and two outer strands around the knot. **Strands braided on a helix** turns three strands five times around a staircase. **The seam of a carried frame** shows something no camera can: carried once around a (3, 4) knot, the frame comes back turned by about 157°. Arms and an arc mark that seam where the loop starts. Choose **Distribute the correction** to spread the opposite twist evenly and close the band, and the note says by how much. Animate **Twist (turns)** or **Angle θ₀** to wring the ribbon, or **N₀** to see the frame turn as a whole. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-frames-offsets-and-ribbons).

## Ruled surfaces and chord families

Join every point of the curve to a partner by a straight thread, and the threads sweep out a ruled surface. Choose **Ruled surface · chords & threads**. The pairing is part of the study: the partner of t sits at parameter mt + δ, either on the same curve, so the threads are chords, or on a second thread b(t) that you write as expressions. Slide **Shift δ** and the rulings cross, pulling a cylinder into a waisted hyperboloid. The shading uses the true surface normal, which turns along each ruling unless the surface is developable, and the note says which.

**A harmonic loom** strings two rippling rings, a harmonic curve and an expression thread, across a phase shift. Without the ripples it would be a hyperboloid, whose rulings never meet. The ripples tilt neighbouring rulings enough that they pass through one another near the waist, so the sheet really does cross itself there; the crossing is drawn, not trimmed. Shrink the ripples to about a third and it separates again. **Chords across the trefoil** joins each point of the knot to the point a third of the way ahead. **Chords of a rising helix** draws half-turn chords through the axis and stops where the partner would pass the end of the helix, counting the chords left out. A constant thread makes a cone, singular at its apex; a zero shift makes every chord a point and leaves no surface. Hide **Ruled surface** to see the threads alone. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-ruled-surfaces-and-chord-families).

## Tubes and canal surfaces

Roll a sphere along the curve, letting its radius change as it goes, and the surface that touches every sphere is a canal surface. Choose **Tube · canal surface**. Each sphere touches the surface along a circle. With a constant radius that circle stands square to the curve and the surface is a tube; as the radius grows, the circle leans back along the tangent and shrinks. Where the radius changes as fast as the centre moves, the circle closes to a point. Faster still, each sphere sits inside the next and there is no surface at all. The note reports each case.

**A tube around the trefoil** wraps the knot in a tube of one radius, with four meridians. Carried once around, the frame that places them returns turned by about 95°, so the meridians meet with a visible seam while the tube itself closes. **A necklace of spheres** swells and narrows the radius along a helix; the contact circles tilt as it changes. **Beads that lose their envelope** runs a quickly varying radius along a straight line. On each steep flank the radius grows faster than the centre moves, so the string breaks into separate beads, each closing to a point. Nothing is trimmed: a tube wider than the curve can turn folds through itself, and the note says where. Animate **Tube radius R** to watch a tube swell until it folds, or a string of beads come apart. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-canal-surfaces-and-tubes).

## Vector-field trajectories

Give every point of space a velocity and a seed dropped anywhere drifts along it: its path, a trajectory, has the field as its tangent at every moment. Choose **Spatial definition · Vector field** and write dx/dt, dy/dt and dz/dt. Each seed's trajectory is integrated on its own, to its own error tolerance, and all are shown at the same times, so a reveal grows them together. A trajectory that leaves the escape sphere, meets a field that is not finite, or runs out of steps stops there and is never continued; the note and a small cross mark where. The first trajectory is the curve a construction builds on, and **None · the curve alone** shows the trajectories by themselves.

**A rising vortex** turns four seeds on a ring into helices with the field (−y, x, a), each checked against its exact formula, and hangs the tangent ribbon from the first. **Lorenz's two wings** starts two seeds a thousandth apart on Lorenz's equations (σ = 10, ρ = a = 28, β = 8/3). They fold around the two unstable equilibria, then separate until they cross between the wings at different times. **A ribbon along Rössler's band** carries a rotation-minimizing ribbon along one of Rössler's trajectories (a = b = 0.2, c = 5.7) as it spirals out from the equilibrium and folds back through its spike. Animate a to move Lorenz's ρ, or a seed coordinate to follow a different trajectory. Chaotic trajectories are sensitive to every rounding, so their fine detail is not reproducible across platforms. Other coefficients need not give chaos. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-vector-field-trajectories).

## Cyclic pursuit

Each pursuer runs straight at the next, the last at the first, at its own speed. Choose **Spatial definition · Pursuit**, place the starts anywhere in space, and set a capture distance: a pursuer's direction is undefined on its target, so the chase stops, for everyone, the first time anyone comes that close to their own. Later samples are left empty, nobody merges or changes target, and the note says who caught whom and when. Grey polygons join the pursuers in chase order at evenly spaced times.

**Four pursuers on a tetrahedron** start at the corners of a regular tetrahedron. A quarter turn with a reflection carries each start to the next, and the chase keeps that symmetry: every pursuer spirals down the same paraboloid, `z = ρ²/√2`, while its shadow on the floor traces a logarithmic spiral. **A chase untangling a trefoil** starts nine equal pursuers along a knot; the knot loosens into twisting spirals as they close in. **A crown of six with a tangent ribbon** raises and lowers alternate corners of a hexagon and hangs the tangent developable from the first pursuer: each tangent points at the pursuer it chases. A regular polygon in any plane gives the planar spirals, checked against their formula. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-cyclic-pursuit).

## Surfaces and their centres of curvature

A surface bends by different amounts in different directions. At each point the most and least it bends are its principal curvatures κ₁ and κ₂, and their centres lie on the normal line, at distances 1/κ₁ and 1/κ₂. As the point moves, those centres sweep two focal sheets. Choose **Spatial definition · Surface patch** and a surface. Its normal lines, offset and focal sheets are drawn in their own layers, with the first focal sheet in rust and the second in slate. A surface is not a curve, so the curve constructions give way to its own controls.

**A torus revealing its centers** opens the outer band of a torus and runs its normals inwards. Every normal crosses the core circle and meets the axis, so both focal sheets collapse to curves: the core circle, and the axis, reached through infinity at the top and bottom of the tube. **A sphere collapsing to one focus** cuts a sphere open to show its normals running to the centre, where both sheets collapse to a point, with an offset sphere inside. **The focal sheets of an ellipsoid** opens one half of a three-axis ellipsoid. Its two sheets of centres are genuine surfaces, folded along cuspidal edges and meeting at the umbilics. **An elliptic cylinder and its evolute** shows the ellipse's evolute swept along the axis; the other sheet lies at infinity, along the straight rulings. Reverse the normal and the curvatures change sign, the sheets swap numbers and colours, and the geometry stays. Animate **Offset d** past a focal sheet to watch the offset turn inside out. The poles of the ellipsoid's chart and the axis of a spindle torus are chart singularities, with no normal there: the note counts them, and focal sheets and offsets stop short of them. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-surfaces-normals-offsets-and-focal-sheets).

## Mirrors and their caustics

Light a surface as a mirror and every ray reflects: R = I − 2(I·n)n. Neighbouring reflected rays cross, or nearly, at up to two places along each ray, the centres of curvature of the reflected wavefront, and those crossings sweep the caustic, the fold where reflected light gathers. Choose **Spatial definition · Mirror · reflected rays**, a surface, and parallel light or a point source. The first caustic branch is drawn in rust and the second in slate. A caustic ahead of the mirror is real: the rays converge through it. One behind the mirror is virtual: the rays only appear to diverge from it, and it is drawn only by its lines, with the rays' extensions behind the mirror.

**A paraboloid gathering light** sends light along the axis through one focus: both branches collapse to it. **A tilted beam folding into coma** tips the same light by 20°, and the focus spreads into two cusped sheets. **A spherical bowl's cusped caustic** shows why a sphere cannot focus: it folds parallel light into a surface whose profile is a nephroid, cusped at the paraxial focus, and a line on the axis. **An ellipsoid refocusing its lamp** places a point source at one focus of an ellipsoid of revolution, and every ray meets again at the other. **A cup's nephroid** slants light into half a cylinder, the three-dimensional cousin of the bright curve in a coffee cup; its second caustic lies at infinity, along the straight rulings. Animate the elevation to sweep the light, or move the lamp off its focus to watch the point open into sheets. **Trace rays** starts from the bare mirror: light streams in from past the framed view, or leaves the lamp, reflects or refracts, and each caustic point appears as its ray reaches it, with rays continuing to their full length and the receiver shown once the family is complete. Light slows to c/n, so the paraboloid's rays all reach its focus at once. Only the two held cameras are offered. The light reflects once, and nothing blocks it, so a ray may pass through another part of the mirror; this is a geometric study, not a rendering of brightness. See [the definitions and checks](mathematics.md#spatial-mirrors-reflected-rays-and-caustics).

## Refraction and receivers

Switch the **Interaction** to refraction and the surface becomes an interface between two media, with index n₁ on the side the normal points to and n₂ beyond: T = ηI + (ηc − √k)n, η = n₁/n₂. **A glass ellipsoid focusing a beam** is Descartes' lens: an ellipsoid whose eccentricity is 1/n brings a parallel beam to its far focus with no aberration at all, so both caustics are one point. **A glass dome's ring of light** shows what a sphere does instead: the rim focuses short of the centre, the caustic flares into a cusped sheet around a line on the axis, and a receiver plane across it catches a bright ring around a fainter disc. **A lamp in water over air** shows total internal reflection: only a cone of the lamp's light escapes, the rest turns back in grey, and what escapes seems to leave a smeared virtual lamp above the surface. **A cup's nephroid on its floor** puts a receiver under the cup's reflected light, where the cusped bright curve of a coffee cup appears.

A receiver measures light, which a caustic does not. Every patch of the surface sends the light it intercepts, and each bin shows the mean irradiance over its square on a logarithmic scale, with every share of the light accounted for in the note. It is a model of flux, not a photograph: nothing is absorbed, there are no Fresnel losses or shadows, and a real caustic's brightness is infinite on a line, so a bin crossed by one is bright but finite. See [the receiver's definition](mathematics.md#spatial-receiver-planes-and-irradiance).

## Implicit surfaces and sections

A surface need not be drawn from parameters: the points where an expression F(x, y, z) takes one value c form a level surface, found by sampling F on a grid inside a box. **A sphere and its latitudes** is the simplest, cut by seven planes in circles of latitude. **The spiric sections of Perseus** cuts a torus with planes parallel to its axis, the curves the Greek geometer Perseus studied: two circles, two ovals, Bernoulli's lemniscate where the plane touches the inner equator, a peanut and an oval. **Villarceau circles** cuts the same torus with a plane tangent to it at two points, in two perfect circles as wide as its core. **Two drops meeting** is the set of points whose distances to two foci multiply to a constant: animate c past 1 and the drops join through a saddle, with the plane z = 0 cutting them in Cassini ovals. **A double torus** thickens a figure eight into a closed surface of genus two, and **the tanglecube** is a cage of genus five that falls apart into eight drops as c falls below −12.5. **A gyroid, cut open** shows a surface that fills space, one period of it, cut open by its box. **A thread between two drops** takes the Cassini drops just past c = 1, where their waist is thinner than a cell. The grid alone sees two drops, with the section at x = 0 floating between them; refinement finds the thread and joins them.

Every section is a planar level set, the curve the 2D notebook draws for F(x, y) = c, and it is traced by the same code. The note reads the mesh's topology as it stands, its pieces and their Euler characteristic, and says where it is uncertain: poles and jumps are marked and never meshed, undefined regions are left open, and grid faces whose corners alternate are counted, since there the grid, not F, decides whether the surface joins. **Refinement levels** halves the cells where a sample between two points of the grid lies on the other side of the level, the sign of a thin neck, a narrow gap, a thread between grid points, or a face split the wrong way. The note says how far it went and what it left. See [the definitions](mathematics.md#spatial-implicit-surfaces-and-sections).

**Export image** saves a 2000 × 1520 PNG or an SVG containing that shaded PNG and study metadata. **Image size** below them chooses 1000 × 760, 2000 × 1520, 3000 × 2280 or 4000 × 3040 for every format, line drawings included. **Transparent background** leaves the background out: the PNG's alpha is what the drawing covers, see-through sheets included ([how](mathematics.md#still-exports-size-and-transparent-background)), and the line drawings have no background rectangle. Files record a size or background only when it is not the default. A size this device's WebGL cannot draw is refused with the limit named. The SVG option is explicitly labelled as an embedded image, since depth-tested shading is raster content.

Its **Lines (SVG)** items save the drawing's lines: true SVG paths, suited to editing or a pen plotter, on the same page, camera, layers and theme as the PNG. Surfaces are not drawn; only the lines of the shown layers are, one group per layer and one path per color. Breaks and gaps in the engine's geometry stay breaks, and lines leaving the page or the drawing's depth range are clipped at its edge. There are two versions, and each file records which one it is:

- **Every line** draws every shown line, including lines behind surfaces.
- **Visible only, sampled** leaves out the parts of lines behind a shown surface. Each line is tested every half pixel against the surface triangles nearest at the surrounding pixel centers, at the page's resolution, with the live drawing's polygon offset, so a line lying on a surface stays visible just as it is drawn. This approximates hidden lines; it is not exact hidden-line removal. A surface folding over itself hides lines as it does on screen, but near a silhouette a line can be cut up to about a pixel early or late. A surface triangle nearest at no pixel center (smaller than a pixel and covered) cannot hide anything, and lines never hide other lines. With every surface hidden, the two versions are the same. The work is bounded: a study needing more than 200 million pixel and line-sample tests reports that instead of saving part of a file. Animation exports use the same sampler and renderer, with MP4 or animated WebP according to browser capabilities, adjustable resolution/quality/frame rate, and exact file duration independent of rendering speed. Cancellation discards the file. No screen recording or external service is involved.

## Probing a curve

**Probe the curve** turns any curve study into an explanation of itself. Its slider picks a point and shows the Frenet frame there (T, N and B in red, green and blue), the osculating circle in magenta, and that point's own construction lines, while the sidebar reads out κ, 1/κ and τ and plots both along the whole curve. On **Helix · a ribbon staircase** they stay at 18/37 and 3/37 everywhere, as a helix's must. On **A knot shedding filaments** the four deepest curvature dips, where κ falls to 0.17 and the knot almost straightens, are exactly where τ spikes to −4: there the osculating plane swings round fastest. On **Beads that lose their envelope** the spheres roll along a straight line, so the curve probe draws no frame and says the curve is straight: it describes the curve a surface is built on, never the surface (see the next section for the surface). The probe is diagnostic: its colors are not the decorative palette, and it never changes the study. With the probe on, **Animate → Move the probe along the curve** sweeps it from the first sample to the last over the unchanged study, in playback and in MP4 or WebP exports. **Vary parameters** keeps it on too, describing each frame as the curve changes shape: **Probe** chooses whether it **Stays at its t**, **Keeps its share of the length**, or **Moves along the curve** while the parameters vary. Four examples show it:

- **A helix pressed through its circle** reverses the helix's pitch. Its three coils each way press into one circle, the osculating circle closes onto them, and τ passes through zero as the helix changes hand.
- **A twisted cubic losing its bend** flattens the bend at its middle, where κ = 2|a| and τ = 3/a: the torsion races off to infinity, the frame whips over, the osculating circle swells out of the view, and at a = 0 the probe reads the point flat, with no frame to draw.
- **A trefoil breathing under a moving probe** swells the knot's tube while the probe rides it out and back.
- **A knot reparameterized in place** changes only the parameterization: rulings flow along a trefoil that never moves. Held by its share of the length, the probe stays at one point with steady κ and τ while its t wanders; choose **Stays at its t** to watch it slide instead.

Other animations leave the probe out, and still images include it.

## Probing a surface

A surface patch, and a canal, tangent developable, framed ribbon or ruled surface built on any curve (choose **Describe → The surface**), can be probed the same way with **Principal curvatures & centres at a point**. Two sliders pick a point on the surface's grid, and the drawing shows its normal, its two principal directions, and the two normal-section circles through it, each centred on its focal point on the normal line. Each branch takes its focal sheet's color, rust and slate. The sidebar reads out both principal curvatures with their radii, and the Gaussian and mean curvatures, and plots both curvatures along the row through the point.

On a canal the branches are its two lines of curvature. **Around the circle**, the curvature is always −1/R, centred on the sphere's centre on the curve, which is why a canal's first focal set is the curve itself. **Across it**, the curvature tells the shape of the envelope:

- On **Beads that lose their envelope**, a bead's widest point at t = π/4 reads −0.7937 and −0.586: both negative, so K is positive and the bead is convex there.
- At a neck, t = −π/4, the curvature across turns positive (3.263), so K is negative: the neck is a saddle, and its second centre lies outside the surface.
- Towards each gap, where the spheres stop having an envelope, the curvature across grows without bound and its plot is pinned to the edge. At t = 0, between the beads, there is no surface at all, and the panel says so.

On a torus patch, κ₁ = −cos v/(R + r cos v) and κ₂ = −1/r, and its centres trace the focal sheets drawn beside it: the core circle and the axis.

The developable, the framed ribbon and the ruled surface are all made of straight lines, and the probe draws the line through the point. A surface made of straight lines can never curve like a ball: K ≤ 0 everywhere on them, and K = 0 exactly where the surface could be unrolled flat.

- On **Helix · a ribbon staircase**, the curvature along each tangent ruling is 0, with its centre at infinity, so K = 0: the ribbon is developable. Across the ruling it is τ/(κ|u|) = 1/(6|u|): 1.333 next to the curve, at u = 0.125, falling to 1/9 at the ribbon's edge, u = ±1.5. As the probe moves in toward the curve its centre rushes onto it: the curve is the ribbon's edge of regression, where its two sheets meet in a cusp, and the probe leaves that line out because the surface has no normal there.
- On **The seam of a carried frame** the rotation-minimizing frame turns only toward the tangent, never about it, so the untwisted band is developable too: K reads 0 wherever the probe stands. On **A band around the trefoil** the frame is twisted twice around the knot, and K turns negative, −0.1714 halfway along the spine. At the spine, K = −Ω², where Ω is how fast the band turns about the curve.
- On **Chords of a rising helix**, each half-turn chord crosses the helix's axis halfway along, at u = 0.5. There K = −9 and H = −π/4 at every t: the chords sweep an oblique helicoid, a saddle everywhere.

## Probing the light

A mirror or interface study probes the light leaving it, with **Wavefront, foci & rays at a point** (or, with **Describe → The mirror** or **The interface**, the surface itself, as a surface study would). The drawing shows the incident ray and the outgoing ray through the point in the probe's normal ink. It also shows the outgoing wavefront's two principal directions and its two normal-section circles, each centred on its focus, in the caustics' rust and slate. Each focus is the point where that ray touches the caustic of the same number: a caustic has two sheets because every ray has two foci, one for each way its wavefront curves. The sidebar reads out μ₁ and μ₂, the signed distance along the ray to each focus (positive for a real focus ahead of the surface, negative for a virtual one behind it), the astigmatic interval between them, and the angle of incidence θ (and, through an interface, the angle of transmission θ′).

- On **A spherical bowl's cusped caustic**, the middle of the grid meets the light at θ = 35°. Its two foci are Coddington's: the tangential one at R cos θ/2 = 0.6144 along the ray, on the nephroid sheet, and the sagittal one at R/(2 cos θ) = 0.9156, on the axis. They are 0.3012 apart. Probing the mirror instead reads κ = 1/R = 0.6667 both ways: a sphere is umbilic everywhere, so the whole astigmatism comes from the oblique light.
- On **A paraboloid gathering light** every point is stigmatic: both foci are the focus, and the interval is 0.
- On **A lamp in water over air**, straight below the lamp the light is stigmatic, imaged virtually 0.7519 above the surface (each focus reads −0.7519), at n₂/n₁ of the lamp's height. Moving out, the two virtual foci separate, and beyond Snell's window the panel reports total internal reflection, with the reflected ray drawn instead.

## Cutting away

Opaque sheets hide what lies inside them: the focal surface inside a closed shell, the rays inside a closed mirror, a caustic sheet in front of its own rays. Earlier examples opened their surfaces by trimming the parameter domain, which also halves the study: half the mirror reflects half the rays. **Cut away → Cut with a plane** keeps the whole study and hides only part of the drawing. Everything on the side the normal points to, where n̂·p > d, is hidden. The plane is fixed in space, so the camera can turn around the opening. **What it cuts** chooses the study's own surface, every sheet, or every sheet and line. **Face the view** points the normal at you, so the near half disappears. **Through the center** passes the plane through the middle of the study, and **Flip** keeps the plane and hides the other side. The edge, where the drawn sheets meet the plane, is drawn in graphite (chalk on the dark background). It follows the mesh: it is not a refined section curve.

- **An ellipsoid hiding its centers** has semi-axes 1.2, 1 and 0.9. Since a² < 2c², every center of curvature lies strictly inside the shell. Closed, the shell hides every pixel of both focal sheets, and turning them off changes nothing. The cut opens the shell's near half along an ellipse, leaves its parameter curves standing as a cage, and shows the whole centro-surface: two sheets folded along cuspidal edges. Choose **Every sheet** to slice the focal sheets too.
- **A lamp sealed in an ellipsoid** is the whole spheroidal mirror of **An ellipsoid refocusing its lamp**, cut lengthwise. Every ray from the lamp at one focus, not half of them, meets again at the other.

With the cut on, **Animate → Peel away with the cut** moves the plane along its normal, from where it hides nothing to where it hides all it cuts. On the ellipsoid it peels the shell from its centers, slice by slice. Flip the normal to peel from the other side. Other animations keep the plane where you put it, and every export draws it as the screen does.

## Seeing through

A cut removes part of the drawing. **See through** keeps all of it. With **Sheets → See-through**, every layer of every shown sheet is drawn at once: a point of the page covered by n layers shows the mean of their shaded colors, laid over the background with opacity 1 − (1 − α)ⁿ. One layer at α = 0.3 covers 30% of the background, two 51%, four 76%, so a fold or a sheet inside another reads as a denser region, like an X-ray. The order of the layers never matters, so sheets that cut through one another, such as focal sheets, caustics and self-intersecting level sets, are drawn exactly as the rule says, with no sorting artifacts. It is a way of seeing folds, not a model of light through glass, and the study itself is unchanged.

**Lines behind sheets** chooses what happens to lines the nearest sheet hides: hidden, as an opaque drawing hides them, faint, or dashed like a textbook's hidden lines. Dashes are measured along the line in space, so a line that recedes from view has shorter dashes on the page, a cue to its depth. Either works with opaque sheets too: the tube around the trefoil, kept opaque with dashed lines, shows the knot running inside it.

- **A Klein bottle passing through itself** is the usual immersion of the Klein bottle as a level set, (x² + y² + z² + 2x − 1)[(x² + y² + z² − 2x − 1)² − 8z²] + 16yz(x² + y² + z² − 2x − 1) = 0 (Stewart, as given by MathWorld, with x and y exchanged so the default view looks along the neck). Drawn opaque it is two smooth lobes and nothing more. Seen through, its neck turns back, pierces the wall and opens into the body: a Klein bottle in space cannot avoid meeting itself. The section planes cross the bottle's axis, so where the neck is inside the body a plane meets both, one curve within the other, and the inner curves, behind the wall, are dashed. The surface meets itself along a curve where ∇F vanishes, and the mesh shows small slits there, where cells cannot resolve the crossing.

Seeing through also opens the earlier studies without cutting them: **An ellipsoid hiding its centers** shows its whole focal surface inside the whole shell, and **The tanglecube** shows its genus-five body as a lattice of overlapping tubes.

The see-through drawing is not multisampled, so the outlines of see-through sheets are not antialiased, and where many tiny triangles meet in one pixel (a chart's pole) the pixel shows the one at its center. It needs half-float render targets; a device without them draws the sheets opaque and says so.

## Hiding a layer

A cut opens part of a sheet and see-through keeps it all; hiding its layer removes it. Every part of a study is its own layer under **Reveal the construction**, so a drawing can keep only what was inside, or what was drawn on, the surface. Some examples open with layers of their own hidden, and the checkboxes show which; choosing an example without them shows every layer again.

- **The whole focal surface of an ellipsoid** is the 1.5 : 1 : 0.7 ellipsoid of **The focal sheets of an ellipsoid**, closed, with its shell and parameter curves hidden. The two sheets of centers show entire, each folded along cuspidal edges in the planes of symmetry. They meet at the centers of the four umbilics, in the xz-plane, where κ₁ = κ₂.
- **A cage of rings round a (3, 4) knot** is a tube of radius 0.3 drawn by its 120 contact circles and six meridians, with the surface and frames hidden. The seam is distributed, so every meridian closes.
- **A Klein bottle in twenty-four slices** is the bottle of **A Klein bottle passing through itself**, drawn by 24 sections across its axis with the surface, planes and box hidden, from the isometric view. Where the neck runs inside the body, one curve lies within another.

## Line weights

The construction lines are the drawing, so their weight matters. **Lines → Weight** draws them as strokes whose width is a share of the page: the curve heaviest, families lighter, construction lines lightest, so the curve stays legible over hundreds of lines, and a large still keeps the look of the live drawing instead of thinning every line to one pixel. **Hairline** is the drawing as it was before weights. Three examples are drawn for it:

- **A cinquefoil strung to its center** projects the origin onto 240 of the (2, 5) knot's tangent lines. The perpendiculars from the pole to each tangent foot form a fan whose density traces the knot's loops; in fine strokes the hub stays a gradient and the knot reads above it, where hairlines merge the hub into one grey patch and lose the knot among the strings.
- **An engraved trefoil tube** draws 150 contact circles and eight meridians on the tube in bold strokes, with **Lines behind sheets → Dashed**, so the far half of every circle and the knot inside the tube appear as dashes, like an engraving or a draftsman's hidden lines. Hairline dashes are a pixel wide and nearly vanish.
- **A helix unwound into a veil** unwinds five involutes from a rising helix with 240 taut strings: the filaments are drawn heavier than the veil of strings between them. Switch to **Hairline** to see every line, filament and string, drawn alike.

Near a sheet's outline a stroke just behind the sheet can show by up to half its width, because sheets are pushed back slightly so strokes lying on them stay whole.

## Flying the camera

**Animate → Fly through key views** moves the camera alone, through views you choose. Frame the drawing as you like, with orbit, pan and zoom, and press **+ Add the drawing's view**; frame the next and add it, up to twelve. **Show** puts the drawing's camera at a view so you can adjust it, and **Set to drawing** takes the adjusted view back. Name the views: the names appear beside the timeline as the camera reaches each view or flies between two, and never in the drawing.

Each view is reached at an equal share of the duration, and at that moment the drawing is exactly the one its manual camera draws. Between views the camera turns the shorter way round; **Turns** on a view adds whole turns on the way there, positive in the direction the orbit animation turns. **Steady** turns, tilts and zooms at a constant rate on each leg; **Smooth** blends the rates across views without overshooting any. The camera turns about the point in the middle of the page (taken on the plane through the study's center that faces you), and zooms straight toward the place you framed. The camera never rolls and stops 4° short of looking straight down or up, as when you drag. Stop and **Back to study** restore your manual camera; the path travels in a copied link, and MP4 and WebP exports fly it at their own frame rate.

- **Viviani's curve, from every side** is the curve where a sphere meets a cylinder through its center that touches it inside (Viviani, 1692). It opens with its flight. End-on it is a circle, the cylinder seen along its axis; a quarter turn later it is a figure-eight, the lemniscate of Gerono; tilting up toward overhead it folds onto a parabola traced there and back. The camera then zooms in on the point where the curve crosses itself, at right angles where the cylinder touches the sphere, and turns once around it, the crossing held in the middle of the page, before pulling back. Its definitions are in [mathematics](mathematics.md#spatial-camera-paths).

A path flies over any study. On **Villarceau circles**, the cutting plane's normal is horizontal, so the camera can face it exactly: a view at yaw 30° and pitch 0 shows the two sections as true circles, and a view a quarter turn on, at yaw 120°, sees the plane edge-on as a line. With **See through** on, both circles stay visible between the two.

### While the geometry moves

The same views can fly while the drawing itself changes. Choose **Draw along the curve**, **Vary parameters**, **Trace rays**, the probe's motion or the peel under **Animate**, then **Animation camera → Fly through key views**: the key views appear under it, edited as above. Each frame is drawn with the camera of its own moment, so a frame the engine computes late still pairs with the camera of its time. The views are taken about the study as drawn when you press play; geometry that grows far beyond it can leave the page. **Orbit the study** turns the camera itself and offers no path.

- **A helix's string, always level** lengthens the string unwound from a helix while the camera circles it at its own level. However the string's length changes, its involute stays in a level plane at the height of the cusp, so from every level side it is one straight line climbing the helix. The camera then rises to look down the axis, where the line opens into the involute of a circle.
- **Following light into a trough** traces light falling straight into half a cylinder. The camera starts over the trough, drops end-on while the light falls and reflects, and holds there as the caustic closes into a nephroid, the coffee-cup curve, from the rims inward. Light reaches the cusp last; the camera ends looking down onto the cusp line, where the folded sheet creases. Definitions are in [mathematics](mathematics.md#spatial-camera-paths).

### Riding a ray

While **Trace rays** sends light into a mirror or interface, **Animation camera → Ride a ray · perspective** follows one ray in perspective, a little behind the head of its light. Choose the ray where two parameter curves cross with **Ray at u** and **Ray at v**; the middle crossing is chosen first. The camera falls with the light, turns at the surface, and goes on along the reflected, refracted or totally reflected ray as drawn, through the points where that ray touches the caustics, until the ray's head reaches its drawn end, where it rests. **Follow distance** sets how far behind it rides and **Turn window** how gradually it turns, both as optical path (length × refractive index) in radii of the framing sphere. The turn is a pitch toward the side the ray turns to: the camera never rolls, and never follows a virtual ray behind the surface. The readout beside the timeline names the ray and whether the camera is on the incident ray, turning, or on the outgoing one, and the caption under the drawing says the view is in perspective. A finished ride hands over its camera: drag, pan or zoom to return to the view you held, in its own projection, or press Home to return to the ray.

- **Riding a ray through coma** rides one ray of the tilted beam into the paraboloid. Off the axis the focus spreads into two caustic sheets, and each reflected ray touches them at two separate points; the camera falls with the light, turns over the mirror, and passes through both before the ray ends. Definitions are in [mathematics](mathematics.md#riding-a-ray).

## Looking through a lens

**Projection**, beside **Rotate view** above the drawing, sets how the manual camera projects. **Orthographic**, the default, draws every distance at one scale, so a shape's proportions can be measured anywhere on the page. **Perspective · narrow, 30°**, **normal, 50°** and **wide, 90°** look through a lens of that angle across the page's shorter side: nearer parts are drawn larger, so depth reads at a glance and front and back can no longer swap. The plane through the middle of the page keeps its size when you switch, so the drawing stays framed. Zoom then moves the eye toward that point rather than magnifying, so a high zoom through the wide lens takes the eye inside the study. Orbit, pan, the keys and **Reset view** work as before, and Reset view keeps the projection. The menu shows the lens in use, exports record it, and a copied link carries it. Animations draw through it, including a flight through key views, which flies the same views through the lens.

**Perspective · chosen angle** takes any whole number of degrees from 1° to 150°, set with **Lens angle**, which appears after the named views under the drawing. It starts from the lens you were looking through, so the drawing does not jump, or from the angle you last chose. Drag it and the plane through the middle of the page holds still while the eye slides toward or away from it: a _dolly zoom_, the film camera's vertigo shot. Narrow angles flatten depth until, near 1°, the drawing all but agrees with orthographic; wide ones put the eye inside the study and stretch the nearest geometry toward the page's edges. A copied link, exports, Reset view and animations keep the angle, and a link names it even while a named lens is shown, ready for when you choose the angle again.

- **A spiral stair, down its well** is a helix six turns long with its tangent ribbon, seen almost straight down its axis through the wide lens. Choose **Orthographic** and every turn lies on the one below: a single ring. Through the lens each turn is drawn smaller the farther it is from the eye, and the stair falls away to a vanishing point.
- **Diving down the stairwell** flies the same stair through the normal lens: from outside, over the top, down inside the turns, and tilting to look across the inner wall, then back out. Zooming in is what carries the eye inside.
- **Inside a trefoil's tube** puts the eye in the knot's central hole and turns it once about the knot's axis, in a seamless loop: the tube passes overhead, beside you and far away.
- **Down a torus's tunnel** stands inside a ring-shaped tube, looking along it through a 140° lens, with its wall seen through. Its rings sweep out past the page's edges and crowd toward the bend, where the far side of the ring glows through. The pan turns with the camera, so turning once carries the eye all the way around the tunnel, in a seamless loop. Drag **Lens angle** down and the eye backs out through the wall until the whole ring is in view, while the ring at the middle of the page keeps its size: a dolly zoom.

Up close, sheets cover the page many times over, and **Lines (SVG) · visible only, sampled** may refuse a view whose hidden-line test exceeds its work limit; **every line** still exports. Dashes behind sheets keep their length in space, so near dashes look longer. Definitions are in [mathematics](mathematics.md#the-manual-camera-in-perspective).

## Looping and back and forth

**Repeat** sets how an animation spends its duration. **Once** plays from the start to the end. **Loop** plays again and again, its end joining its start, and **Back and forth** plays to the end and back within the duration, again and again. **Pace → Ease in and out** starts and ends the motion slowly, on a half cosine; back and forth then slows to rest at each turn. A repeating animation plays until you pause or stop it, and its export loops forever as an animated WebP and leaves out the last frame, which would show the first one twice at the seam.

A loop is seamless only if its last frame is its first, so pressing play draws both ends and compares them: the drawing sample for sample, the probe and the camera. If they differ, the message under the playback controls says how: by how much of the study's radius the drawing moves, that its pieces differ, that the probe ends elsewhere, or that the camera does. An orbit always returns. Parameter tracks return when each runs one period of the drawing, such as `a` from 0 to `2*pi` in an expression with `cos(t + a)`; a camera path returns when its last view is its first, and **+ Return to view 1** adds that view; the probe returns around a closed curve. Drawing, tracing and peeling start one way and end another, so they offer back and forth instead. A smooth camera path in a loop passes through its joining view without stopping.

- **Beads running around a trefoil** carries nine swelling spheres around the knot by running the curve's parameter on by `a`, while the radius stays fixed in t. As `a` runs once around, each swelling changes shape with the knot's speed and comes back to its place as the camera circles once and returns to its first view: two motions that both close.
- **A Schwarz surface breathing** moves the level of cos x + cos y + cos z through −1.6 cos a. Caps about the corners open into Schwarz's P labyrinth, close to a single drop at the center, and open and close again. It uses cos a rather than sin a because cos(2π) is exactly 1 in floating point, while sin(2π) is not exactly 0, and at a = 0 this surface passes exactly through grid points, where that rounding changes the mesh.
- **A gyroid peeled and regrown** peels the gyroid along the cube's diagonal and lets it grow back, easing at each end. A peel cannot loop, since it starts with everything shown and ends with everything hidden; back and forth repeats it without a jump. Definitions are in [mathematics](mathematics.md#seamless-loops).

## Deliberate limits

The notebook offers torus-knot, expression, and harmonic curves, vector-field trajectories, and cyclic pursuits, with tangent developables, involutes, tangent-foot projections, tangent-line orthotomics, sphere inversions, framed ribbons, ruled surfaces, and canal surfaces, or the curve alone, and analytic surface patches with their normals, offsets and focal sheets or lit as mirrors or interfaces with their reflected or refracted rays, caustics and receiver planes, and implicit level surfaces with their sections, with no transparency, spectral or physical rendering, lighting editor, or saved-camera links. A single cut plane, not a set of clipping planes or a cutaway box, opens closed sheets, and it hides the drawing without changing the study. Rendering needs WebGL; the 2D notebook remains independent. Opaque, double-sided sheets and depth-tested linework make the folds readable. Occlusion hides rear geometry while surfaces are enabled; hiding surfaces exposes the line family. Orthographic projection, the default, avoids perspective scale distortion; **Projection** offers three perspective lenses and a chosen angle for the manual camera and shows the one in use, and the camera that rides a ray draws in perspective and says so in the caption. Both notebooks share one page layout: the same header, sidebar, drawing frame with its heading and legend, and explanation. On desktop the controls scroll independently; narrow screens place the artwork first, followed by the controls and then the explanation.

The engine retains the singular spine and self-intersections, leaves gaps at invalid points and unreliable normals, and closes analytic torus knots exactly. Arbitrary expressions are sampled with bounded numerical derivatives; narrow folds or rapid oscillations may need more samples, a smaller domain, or refining between samples, which judges each chord at three points and can still miss a feature narrower than they are. Refinement draws the curves and involutes more finely, not the surfaces or other constructions built on them. Trajectories are sampled at evenly spaced times, however fast they move, so a fast excursion is drawn with fewer points; constructions are built on the first trajectory or pursuer only, and a spatial field has no direction lattice. A pursuit's connecting polygons share the construction's line count. Surfaces are the five analytic patches only, with no surface expressions, sampled on a uniform grid; a focal sheet's centres beyond 100 surface radii are treated as at infinity, and a sheet is classified as a whole, not region by region. A mirror or interface acts once, with no occlusion, second bounce, reflected share of refracted light, or Fresnel weights; its caustic is a geometric set, not a brightness, and a receiver's irradiance is a bin-averaged flux model on an axis-aligned plane. The default camera views a receiver obliquely, so a small window can need zooming in. An implicit surface is meshed on a grid of at most 262,144 cells and 200,000 triangles, with vertices exact on the surface. Its topology is only as fine as the grid and up to three levels of refinement where samples disagree with it, within 100,000 tetrahedra; refinement finds what its samples see, and certifies nothing. The mesh is a picture, not a differentiable surface, and feeds no curvature or optics. A tilted section stops within one of its cells of the box. No finite-resolution certification is claimed. See [the mathematical conventions](mathematics.md#spatial-tangent-developable).

Analytic derivative checks, torus and speed identities, ruling length/tangency, opposite sheet normals, closure, zero-curvature gaps, convergence, bounded inputs, and the WASM bridge verify the mathematics. Browser checks cover custom expressions, scalars, playback endpoints and cameras, image dimensions, independently decoded animation timing, cancellation, layers, themes, and responsive paired-field layout.

The involute reuses the space curves, sampling, camera, and media path, and gives its filaments and strings their own result type.
